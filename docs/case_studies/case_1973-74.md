# Case Study: The 1973-74 Crash

Landon Lackner · Project Swim, Phase 2B · October 2026

## Summary

From December 1972 to September 1974, US stocks lost **55% after inflation** (47% before it) over 21 months, the worst real fall since the 1930s. It was an inflation crash: prices rose 19% during the fall, the Fed pushed rates to 12.9% instead of cutting, and bonds lost money too. The only assets that protected investors were real ones: commodities (+132% after inflation) and gold (+100%).

**For Project Swim**, this is the worst case for our 55/45 portfolio: it would have lost **40% after inflation** and taken **12 years** to get back to even. The clearest warning signs at the peak were not inflation (which had cooled to about 3%) but record borrowing and expensive favourite stocks; the shock that turned an expensive market into a crash, the oil embargo, came ten months after the peak.

Numbers are from our case-study script (scripts/phase2b/case\_study.py, run 2026-10-11, all data checks passed); history and quotes are from the sources listed at the end.

*The stock fall began about ten months before the oil shock.*

**The setup (1971-72).** In August 1971 Nixon ended the dollar's link to gold and froze wages and prices. The controls held reported inflation down while money growth stayed fast, and 1972 was a boom: real growth of about 7%, inflation under 3%. Investors crowded into the "Nifty Fifty", fifty large growth companies such as Polaroid, Avon and Xerox that were treated as safe at any price; the group averaged about 42 times earnings against 19 for the market. The Dow closed above 1,000 for the first time in November 1972.

**The fall (1973-74).** Stocks peaked in January 1973. Inflation was already climbing back, above 7% by late summer 1973, as the controls were loosened. In October 1973 Arab oil producers imposed an embargo and oil prices roughly quadrupled; a recession began in November. Controls ended in April 1974 and inflation passed 10%. The Fed raised rates to 12.9% in July 1974, Nixon resigned in August, and in October Franklin National, the 20th-largest US bank, failed after currency-trading losses. Stocks bottomed in early October 1974 (the Dow made its final low in December).

**The slow part.** Nothing in this crash was a single day's panic: it was 21 months of mostly small daily losses, with inflation quietly eating whatever was left.

## Cause and effect, step by step

*The chain, in one line: easy money (1970-72) → credit boom (1972) → expensive favourite stocks (1972) → inflation comes back (1973) → rates rise and prices start falling (1973) → oil shock (Oct 1973) → the Fed squeezes (1974) → the bottom (autumn 1974). Fragility built in the first three steps; the next three set it off.*

**In plain language:**

1. **Too much money.** Through the late 1960s the Fed put jobs ahead of stable prices, and in 1971-72 President Nixon pressed Fed chair Arthur Burns to keep money easy before the 1972 election. Money grew about 8% in 1972, much faster than the economy could grow. *Effect:* a boom, and inflation stored up for later.
2. **Hiding the symptom.** In August 1971 Nixon cut the dollar's link to gold (removing the last outside limit on printing money) and froze wages and prices. The freeze made inflation look lower, but economists later estimated it held prices down only 3-4%, and nearly all of that came back once controls ended. *Effect:* people felt safe while the pressure built.
3. **Borrowing and paying up.** Easy money meant easy credit: bank lending grew 12% in 1972, mortgage lending hit a record, and in early 1973 business loans grew at a 39% annual rate. Our data shows private debt at a record and margin loans up 65% in two years. Investors paid 40-95 times earnings for the favourite stocks. *Effect:* a market that could only stay up if nothing went wrong.
4. **Inflation returns.** In 1973 controls were loosened just as world food and raw-material prices doubled. Inflation went from about 3% to over 7% within months. *Effect:* interest rates rose.
5. **Why rising rates sink prices.** A bond or a stock is worth today's value of the money it will pay in future. When rates rise, future money is worth less today, so prices fall. The further in the future the payoff, the bigger the fall: long bonds and high-priced growth stocks (whose profits are mostly far in the future) fell most. *Effect:* the slide began in January 1973, before any crisis.
6. **The oil shock.** In October 1973 Arab producers cut supply and oil went from $2.90 to $11.65 a barrel. That raised costs everywhere and cut spending at the same time: recession plus more inflation ("stagflation").
7. **The squeeze.** With inflation over 10%, the Fed raised its rate to 12.9% in July 1974 instead of cutting. Anyone who had borrowed short-term to hold long-term assets broke: construction-lending REITs, Franklin National Bank, retailers like W.T. Grant. Watergate and Nixon's resignation added uncertainty. *Effect:* forced selling and the final leg down.
8. **The release.** Once rates peaked and the Fed began cutting in late 1974, stocks jumped 29% after inflation in a year. But the underlying cause, too much money, was not fixed, so inflation returned in 1977-80 and the real recovery took ten years.

## Patterns to look for in the other crashes

These are the candidate patterns from 1973-74. Each one is a hypothesis until the next five cases confirm or break it; we score them in the final synthesis.

| # | Pattern | What it looked like in 1973-74 | What would break it in another crash |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Crash = fragility × trigger.** Fragility builds slowly and is visible; the trigger is a surprise | Record debt and pricey favourites (visible); oil embargo (surprise) | A big crash with no visible build-up |
| 2 | **Easy money → credit boom → overpriced favourites** | Money +8%, bank credit +12%, Nifty Fifty at 42x | A crash preceded by tight money and modest prices |
| 3 | **The Fed's response depends on inflation.** Low inflation: it rescues. High inflation: it tightens into the fall | Rates to 12.9% during the crash; only the banking system got rescued | The Fed easing early in a high-inflation crash |
| 4 | **"Duration" decides who loses most.** Anything whose value depends on money far in the future (long bonds, high-priced growth stocks) falls most when rates rise | 20y bonds -27% vs 1y -6%; software, healthcare, restaurants -76 to -84%; miners best | Long-dated assets holding up in a rising-rate crash |
| 5 | **Borrowing short to hold long breaks when rates jump** | Construction REITs, Franklin National, W.T. Grant | Short-funded firms surviving a rate spike |
| 6 | **What protects depends on the crash type.** Inflation crash: real assets. Growth crash: Treasuries | Commodities +132%, gold +100%, Treasuries all negative | Bonds protecting in an inflation crash |
| 7 | **The bottom comes when the policy squeeze peaks, and the first year after is the best** | Bottom as rates peaked; +29% real the next year | Bottoms long after policy turns |
| 8 | **Inflation hides losses** | Back to even in 4 years on price, 10 after inflation | (Only applies to inflationary crashes) |

Pattern 4 is the most useful single idea here: a high-priced growth stock behaves like a long bond, because most of its value is profits expected many years from now. That is why the Nifty Fifty and long Treasuries fell together, and it is the link to watch in today's market.

*Update after the 2022 case study:* patterns 1-5 held again. Pattern 6 held only for Treasuries: gold lost 14% after inflation in 2022, so gold looks like a hedge against falling real interest rates rather than against inflation itself. Pattern 7 was revised: the bottom came when one of the two squeezes visibly turned (the policy rate in 1974, inflation in 2022).

## Before: what the warning signs showed at the peak

Each signal is read at the December 1972 peak using data that was roughly published by then, and ranked against its own history up to that month (100% = the highest ever recorded at the time).

| Signal | At the peak | 2 years earlier | Rank vs history | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Private credit / GDP | 93.7% | 90.2% | **100%** | Record borrowing |
| Margin loans / GDP | 1.04% | 0.63% | **96%** | Borrowing to buy stocks near a record, up 65% in two years |
| Credit spread (Baa minus 10y) | 1.57 pts | 2.73 pts | 85% | Narrowing fast as lenders relaxed, though still wider than most of 1953-72 |
| Fed funds rate | 5.33% | 4.90% | 82% | Rising, and already high for 1954-72 |
| Shiller CAPE | 18.7 | 15.9 | 78% | Expensive, not extreme; the favourites were far pricier (below) |
| CPI inflation | 3.4% | 5.6% | 70% | **Had cooled**, helped a little by price controls |
| Core PCE inflation | 3.1% | 4.8% | 61% | Same |
| Unemployment | 5.3% | 5.9% | 62% | Falling: the economy looked healthy |
| Profit margin (profits / GDP) | 6.0% | 5.2% | 32% | Low by history, so earnings were not obviously inflated |
| Yield curve (10y minus 3m) | +1.15 pts | +1.39 pts | 66% | **Not inverted** at any point in the two years before |

**What this says.**

- **The loudest signal was leverage, not inflation.** Private credit was at a record and margin borrowing had grown 65% in two years. That is what makes a fall deep once something goes wrong.
- **Inflation was quiet at the peak, but the pressure behind it was not.** Money had grown 8% in 1972 and price controls shaved a few points off reported prices; inflation came back above 7% within months. A rule that waited for high inflation (like our watch-only Trigger 1, at 3.25% core PCE) would not have fired before the peak.
- **The market as a whole was expensive but not wildly so** (CAPE 18.7). The bubble was concentrated in the favourite stocks: the Nifty Fifty averaged about 42 times earnings, and Polaroid traded near 95 times.
- **The yield curve gave no warning.** This is one case where the most famous recession signal stayed silent.
- **The caution on all of this:** these are today's revised figures, and one crash is one data point. Test 2B-B checks whether these signals predict crashes across 16 countries.

## During: who won and who lost

| Asset | Return after inflation, Dec 1972 to Sep 1974 |
| --- | --- |
| Commodities (futures) | +131.5% |
| Gold | +99.5% |
| Cash (T-bills) | -4.7% |
| Treasuries 1y | -6.3% |
| **Treasuries 7-10y (ours)** | **-14.9%** |
| Treasuries 20y | -27.2% |
| REITs | -42.6% |
| US stocks | -55.1% |

*Source: our case-study script (Ken French, FRED yields, World Bank gold, AQR commodities, FTSE Nareit), deflated by CPI.*

**Depth and speed.** Stocks fell 47% in price terms and 55% after inflation, because prices rose 19% over the 21 months. Most of the damage was slow: almost half the real loss came in the last six months, from April to September 1974, as inflation passed 10% and the Fed kept raising rates.

**Bonds made it worse, not better.** Every Treasury maturity lost money after inflation, and the longer the bond, the bigger the loss: 20-year bonds lost 27%, our 7-10 year bonds 15%, one-year bonds 6%. Rising yields cut bond prices, and inflation ate the interest. This is the opposite of 2008, when Treasuries rose while stocks fell.

**Real assets won.** Commodities more than doubled after inflation, driven by oil and food prices. Gold doubled. Two caveats: Americans could not legally own gold until the end of 1974, and there were no commodity funds an ordinary investor could buy. These were real price moves, but few portfolios could have held them.

**Industries (Ken French 49, after inflation).** The best five were all things you dig out of the ground: gold mining (+13%, the only industry that gained), steel (-18%), coal (-21%), other mining (-22%) and paper (-32%). The worst five were the expensive growth and consumer favourites: healthcare services (-84%), software (-79%), real estate (-79%), entertainment (-78%) and restaurants (-76%). One check still to do: healthcare services and software were small industries in 1972 with few companies, so a handful of stocks may drive those numbers.

## Policy response: the Fed raised rates into the crash

|  | Fed funds rate | Federal budget (% of GDP) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A year before the peak (Dec 1971) | 4.14% | -2.0 (fiscal 1971) |
| At the peak (Dec 1972) | 5.33% |  |
| Highest (Jul 1974) | **12.92%** |  |
| At the bottom (Sep 1974) | 11.34% | -0.4 (fiscal 1974) |
| A year after the bottom (Sep 1975) | 6.24% | -3.2 (fiscal 1975) |

**The Fed tightened while stocks were falling.** Under Arthur Burns it more than doubled the funds rate between the peak and July 1974, because inflation was the bigger threat. It cut only once the recession was deep, near the bottom. Government spending did not cushion the fall either: the deficit was smaller in fiscal 1974 than before the crash, and only widened in fiscal 1975, after the bottom.

**One rescue did happen, in the banking system.** When Franklin National failed in October 1974, the Fed had lent it $1.75 billion, the largest loan to a member bank up to then, and the FDIC arranged a takeover so depositors were protected. The Fed stopped a bank run; it did not try to stop the stock market falling.

**Why it matters today.** Since 1987 investors have learned to expect the Fed to cut rates when markets fall (the "Fed put"). That expectation only holds when inflation is low. In an inflation crash the Fed's job is to fight prices, so the help arrives late, after the damage is done. 2022 was a milder repeat of the same pattern.

*Full monthly series: data/phase2b/case_1973-74_wealth.csv. 55/45 got back to 1.00 only in December 1984.*

**The bonds barely helped.** 55/45 cut the loss from 55% to 40%, but only because it held fewer stocks, not because the bonds rose: they lost 15% after inflation themselves. In 2008 the same mix lost 26%, with bonds rising to soften the fall. In 1973-74 there was nothing to soften it.

**It stayed underwater for a very long time.** Five years after the bottom, in September 1979, 55/45 was still 22% below its starting value after inflation. It got back to even only in December 1984, twelve years after the peak.

| Never | -37.0% |

Rebalancing slightly hurt here, because each month it bought more stocks on the way down. The gap is under three points, so how often we rebalance is a small decision next to what we hold.

## After: recovery and what came next

|  | Back to the old peak | Time from the peak |
| --- | --- | --- |
| US stocks, before inflation | Dec 1976 | 4.0 years |
| US stocks, after inflation | Mar 1983 | **10.2 years** |
| 55/45, after inflation | Dec 1984 | **12.0 years** |

**Inflation hid the damage.** On a price chart, stocks recovered in four years. After inflation it took more than ten, because prices kept rising about 6-11% a year for the rest of the decade. A 1970s investor who looked only at their statement thought they were fine by the end of 1976; in what their money could buy, they were still about 25% down.

**Buying near the bottom paid well.** Returns after the September 1974 low, per year, after inflation:

|  | 1 year | 3 years | 5 years |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| US stocks | +29.4% | +14.8% | +10.7% |
| 55/45 | +14.2% | +9.3% | +5.2% |

The first year was the best, as it usually is after a crash: the fastest gains come when things still look worst. But the strong start did not last. Inflation came back in 1977-80, and real returns stalled until the Fed under Paul Volcker broke inflation in the early 1980s.

## Companies: who collapsed, who held up, and why

**Collapsed or nearly did:**

| Company | What happened | What did it in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Polaroid | Fell more than 90%; the stock went from 149 to 23½ | **Price.** A real, growing company bought at about 95 times earnings |
| Avon | Fell more than 85% | **Price**, then inflation squeezing its customers |
| W.T. Grant (retailer) | Bankrupt in 1975 | **Earnings without cash.** Its operations used more cash than they made for a decade, while reported profits looked steady and the stock sold at about 20 times earnings in 1973 |
| Franklin National Bank | Failed in October 1974, the 20th-largest US bank; lost $63 million in five months | **Risky loans and currency bets**, funded with short-term money |
| Construction-lending REITs | Many failed in 1974-75 | **Borrowed short to lend long.** They borrowed from banks at 2-3 points over prime; when prime went from 6-7% to 10-12%, the spread turned into losses and borrowers walked away from half-built projects |
| Equity Funding (life insurer) | Bankrupt in April 1973 | **Fraud**: more than 60,000 fake insurance policies |
| GEICO (car insurer) | Lost $126.5 million in 1975; stock fell from $42 to $5 by 1976; rescued by other insurers and a share sale | **Grew too fast** into the 1973-75 recession |

**Held up (relatively):** companies that dig things out of the ground. In our industry data, gold miners were the only industry to gain after inflation (+13%), and steel, coal and other mining lost about 20%, against 55% for the market. They sell at whatever the commodity costs, so inflation raised their revenue. Specific company examples still need checking against filings before we name them.

**The long-run twist.** Most of the favourites fell hard in the crash, but they were not all bad buys. Jeremy Siegel later worked out what each was worth given how it actually grew: Philip Morris was worth about 68 times its 1972 earnings, but investors paid 24; Polaroid was worth about one-eighth of what investors paid. A great company and a great investment are different things, and the difference is the price.

**What separated winners from losers, as rules we can use in Phase 3:**

1. **Price relative to growth.** Polaroid at 95 times earnings needed decades of perfect growth to justify it.
2. **Pricing power in inflation.** Firms that could raise prices with inflation survived; firms whose costs rose faster than their prices were squeezed. Insurers are the clearest case: policies priced at last year's prices, claims paid at this year's.
3. **How the company funds itself.** Short-term borrowing behind long-term assets breaks when rates jump (the REITs, Franklin National).
4. **Cash flow, not just earnings.** W.T. Grant's profits looked fine; its cash flow had been warning for a decade.
5. **Honest accounts.** Fraud is rare, but it costs everything (Equity Funding).

**Were insurers hit hardest?** Two of the decade's most famous collapses were insurers (Equity Funding and GEICO). But GEICO's crisis came in 1975-76, after the stock bottom, and Equity Funding was fraud, not the crash. The real test is the insurance industry's return over the fall, which our script didn't print; that number is still to check.

## What experts said at the time

This section is the check against hindsight: a warning only counts if someone could see it and argued it before the fall.

| Who, when | What they said | How it turned out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wall Street, 1972 | The Nifty Fifty were "one-decision stocks": buy them and never sell, whatever the price | **Wrong.** Polaroid fell 90%, Avon 85% |
| The White House, January 1973 (Economic Report of the President) | "1973 will be another very good year for the American economy"; both unemployment and inflation "should be reduced further" | **Wrong on both.** Inflation passed 7% by late summer 1973; recession began in November |
| Shadow Open Market Committee (monetarist critics of the Fed), September 1973 | Inflation "is at the highest rate in peacetime history and shows no sign of ending"; warned against sudden swings in policy | **Right on persistence, wrong on size.** They expected 4-5% inflation on the GNP price measure; 1974 came in around 9% on that measure and 12% on the CPI |
| Federal Reserve under Arthur Burns, 1974 | Tightened hard to fight inflation, then eased in 1975 under political pressure and weak early data | **Mixed.** Later research finds the first reports showed inflation lower and the recession deeper than they really were, which pushed the Fed to ease too soon. Inflation returned in 1977-80 |
| James Dines, newsletter writer, July 1974 | "This is a Crash. And the worst was yet to come." | **Right.** Stocks fell about another fifth after inflation in the next two months |

**The pattern.** The official forecasters and the crowd were confidently wrong at the peak. The people who were right argued from money and inflation (the monetarists), and even they badly underestimated how far inflation would go. Nobody we found predicted the oil embargo, which turned an expensive, over-borrowed market into a crash. The useful lesson is not "someone saw it coming" but that **in 1972 the risks were visible (leverage, valuations, suppressed inflation), even though the trigger was not.**

## Lessons

**For an investor today**

1. **Judge everything after inflation.** The 1973-74 crash looks like a four-year setback on a price chart and a ten-year one in real terms. Any record, fund or backtest quoted only in dollars can hide this.
2. **Watch borrowing more than inflation at the top.** At the peak, inflation looked tame even though fast money growth had already set it up; record credit and margin debt were the real fragility.
3. **In an inflation crash, bonds are not a hedge and the Fed is not coming.** Bonds fell with stocks, longer bonds fell most, and the Fed raised rates into the fall. Protection came only from real assets.
4. **A great company at any price is not safe.** The Nifty Fifty were mostly excellent businesses; buying them at 40-95 times earnings is what failed.
5. **The trigger is usually a surprise; the fragility is not.** Nobody forecast the oil embargo, but the over-borrowed, over-priced market it hit was visible.

**For Project Swim (decisions stay with the pre-registered tests; nothing changes yet)**

- **State the worst case honestly.** The README used to say the portfolio "accepts that bonds will not protect it in an inflation shock like 2022". 2022 cost 55/45 about 25% after inflation (see the 2022 case study); 1973-74 would have cost 40% and 12 years. Done on 2026-10-16: the README now states the 1973-74 worst case, so the risk we accept is stated at its worst rather than its mildest.
- **Test 2B-A matters more after this case.** Commodities (+132%) and gold (+100%) were the only protection. 2B-A tests whether a capped 10% position (trend-following, commodities or REITs) protects across inflation crashes at an acceptable cost; REITs already look weak here (-43%).
- **Test 2B-F (bond maturity) got a first data point:** shorter bonds lost far less (1-year -6% vs 20-year -27%). One crash is not a rule; the test will check growth crashes too, where long bonds usually win.
- **Phase 3 screening:** the five company rules above (price vs growth, pricing power, funding, cash flow, honest accounts) are candidates for the stock screen.
- **Rebalancing:** monthly, yearly or never changed the loss by under three points. Keep monthly; it is not worth more study.
- **Warning-sign test 2B-B:** record private credit and margin debt are the signals to test first across 16 countries; the yield curve gave no warning here.

## Data, method and limits

**Method.** The numbers come from scripts/phase2b/case\_study.py (committed before it ran, with 41 known-answer tests), run on 2026-10-11. The peak and bottom are the highest and lowest month-end levels of US stocks' total return after inflation inside a window fixed in advance (June 1972 to December 1975). All data checks passed: yield-based bond estimates matched real funds (correlation 0.98 and 0.99), AQR commodity returns matched the S&P GSCI (0.83), and every FRED series title was printed and checked.

**Sources of data.** Stocks and industries: Ken French Data Library. Rates, inflation, credit, profits, budget: FRED (St. Louis Fed). Valuation: Robert Shiller's CAPE. Commodities: AQR Century of Factor Premia. Gold: World Bank. REITs: FTSE Nareit All Equity REITs.

**Limits.**

- **One crash.** Patterns here are hypotheses until the other cases and the 16-country tests confirm them.
- **Revised data.** Warning signs use today's revised figures, lagged to roughly when they were published, not what people actually saw at the time.
- **Inflation is slightly overstated** before 1983 (CPI then counted mortgage rates in housing costs), so the 1970s real losses look a little worse than they were.
- **Gold** uses monthly average prices before 2004, which smooth moves; Americans could not legally own it until 31 December 1974.
- **Commodities** were not investable for ordinary investors in 1973; the AQR series is a research index of futures.
- **Bond returns before 2002** are estimated from yields with a simple model that slightly understates big rallies.
- **Company examples** come from secondary sources and should be checked against filings before any is used in an interview.
